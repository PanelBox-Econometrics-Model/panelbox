# Basel III Framework Overview

## Introduction

Basel_III is the most comprehensive international regulatory framework for banking supervision, developed and issued by the BCBS (Basel Committee on Banking Supervision). Basel_III establishes global standards for bank capital adequacy, liquidity, and leverage, aiming to strengthen the resilience of the banking sector following the 2007-2009 global financial crisis. The BCBS, headquartered in Basel, Switzerland, serves as the primary international standard-setting body for the prudential regulation of banks, providing a forum for cooperation on banking supervisory matters.

## Historical Evolution: From Basel I to Basel III

### Basel I (1988)

The original Basel Capital Accord, known as Basel I, was introduced in 1988 by the BCBS. It established the first internationally agreed minimum capital requirements for banks, focusing primarily on Credit_Risk. Basel I introduced a simple framework requiring banks to hold capital equal to at least 8% of their RWA (Risk-Weighted Assets). While groundbreaking at the time, Basel I was criticized for its overly simplistic risk-weighting categories and its failure to account for Market_Risk and Operational_Risk.

### Basel_II (2004)

Basel_II was published by the BCBS in June 2004 as a more risk-sensitive replacement for Basel I. Basel_II introduced the now-familiar three-pillar structure: Pillar_1 (minimum capital requirements), Pillar_2 (supervisory review process), and Pillar_3 (market discipline). Basel_II significantly expanded the scope of risk coverage to include Operational_Risk alongside Credit_Risk and Market_Risk. It also introduced the IRB (Internal Ratings-Based) approach, allowing qualifying banks to use their own internal models to estimate PD (Probability of Default), LGD (Loss Given Default), and EAD (Exposure at Default) for Credit_Risk capital calculations. The SA (Standardised Approach) remained available for banks that did not adopt IRB.

### Basel_III (2010 Onwards)

Basel_III was developed by the BCBS in direct response to the deficiencies revealed by the 2007-2009 financial crisis. The crisis exposed critical weaknesses in bank capital quality, excessive leverage, inadequate liquidity buffers, and procyclical deleveraging. Basel_III retains the three-pillar structure from Basel_II but introduces substantially higher capital requirements, new liquidity standards, a non-risk-based Leverage_Ratio, and macroprudential elements such as capital buffers.

## The Three Pillars of Basel III

### Pillar_1: Minimum Capital Requirements

Pillar_1 sets the quantitative minimum capital requirements that all internationally active banks must meet. Under Basel_III, Pillar_1 covers three major risk categories:

- **Credit_Risk**: The risk of loss arising from a borrower's failure to repay. Banks calculate Credit_Risk capital using either the SA or the IRB approach, with IRB further divided into IRB_Foundation and IRB_Advanced. Key risk parameters under IRB include PD, LGD, and EAD, which feed into the Expected_Loss formula: Expected_Loss = PD * LGD * EAD.

- **Market_Risk**: The risk of losses in on- and off-balance-sheet positions arising from movements in market prices. Basel_III introduced the FRTB (Fundamental Review of the Trading Book) to overhaul Market_Risk capital requirements, incorporating VaR (Value at Risk) and CVA (Credit Valuation Adjustment) charges.

- **Operational_Risk**: The risk of loss resulting from inadequate or failed internal processes, people, systems, or external events. Basel_III introduced the SMA (Standardised Measurement Approach) to replace the previous BIA (Basic Indicator Approach) and other methods for Operational_Risk capital.

The CAR (Capital Adequacy Ratio) under Pillar_1 is calculated as total regulatory capital divided by RWA. Basel_III requires a minimum CAR that combines CET1, AT1, and Tier_2 capital with various buffers including the CCB (Capital Conservation Buffer), CCyB (Countercyclical Capital Buffer), G_SIB_Surcharge, and D_SIB_Buffer.

### Pillar_2: Supervisory Review Process

Pillar_2 requires banks to conduct their own internal assessments of capital adequacy relative to their risk profiles. The key processes under Pillar_2 include:

- **ICAAP** (Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process): Banks must develop comprehensive internal processes to assess their capital needs in relation to all material risks, including those not fully captured under Pillar_1. The ICAAP must incorporate forward-looking Stress_Testing to evaluate capital adequacy under adverse conditions.

- **SREP** (Supervisory Review and Evaluation Process): Supervisors evaluate each bank's ICAAP, risk management frameworks, and internal controls. The SREP may result in additional capital requirements beyond the Pillar_1 minimums.

- **IRRBB** (Interest Rate Risk in the Banking Book): Under Pillar_2, banks must assess and manage the risk arising from interest rate movements affecting their banking book positions.

Pillar_2 also addresses concentration risk, liquidity risk beyond the Pillar_1 metrics, and reputational risk, among others. Supervisory authorities, such as the BCB (Banco Central do Brasil) in Brazil, are responsible for implementing Pillar_2 requirements in their jurisdictions.

### Pillar_3: Market Discipline

Pillar_3 promotes transparency and market discipline by requiring banks to disclose information about their risk exposures, risk assessment processes, and capital adequacy. BCBS_239 (Principles for Effective Risk Data Aggregation and Risk Reporting) is closely linked to Pillar_3, establishing standards for how banks aggregate and report risk data. Key Pillar_3 disclosures include:

- Capital composition and adequacy ratios (CET1, Tier_1, Tier_2, CAR)
- RWA by risk category (Credit_Risk, Market_Risk, Operational_Risk)
- Leverage_Ratio disclosures
- LCR (Liquidity Coverage Ratio) and NSFR (Net Stable Funding Ratio) disclosures
- Counterparty Credit_Risk exposures, including SA_CCR (Standardised Approach for Counterparty Credit Risk)
- Remuneration policies

## BCBS Role and Governance

The BCBS is the primary body responsible for developing Basel_III standards. Membership includes central banks and bank supervisors from 28 jurisdictions, including the BCB from Brazil. The BCBS does not have formal supranational authority; its standards are implemented through national legislation and regulation by member jurisdictions. The BCBS works closely with G_SIB (Global Systemically Important Banks) designations, identifying institutions whose failure could pose systemic risks and requiring them to hold additional capital through the G_SIB_Surcharge.

## Implementation Timeline

Basel_III implementation has occurred in phases:

- **2013**: Initial implementation of higher CET1 and Tier_1 capital requirements began.
- **2015**: Introduction of the LCR requirement with a phased minimum starting at 60%, reaching 100% by 2019.
- **2016**: Pillar_3 disclosure requirements were revised and strengthened.
- **2018**: The NSFR requirement became effective, requiring a minimum ratio of 100%.
- **2018**: The Leverage_Ratio minimum of 3% was formally introduced.
- **2019**: Full phase-in of the CCB at 2.5%.
- **2023 onwards**: Basel III final reforms (sometimes called "Basel 3.1" or "Basel IV") began implementation, including the revised SA for Credit_Risk, output floors for IRB models, the FRTB for Market_Risk, and the SMA for Operational_Risk.

## Relationship with Other Regulatory Frameworks

Basel_III interacts with several other regulatory frameworks. In the United States, the Dodd_Frank Act incorporates many Basel_III principles into domestic law. The SOX (Sarbanes-Oxley Act) and COSO framework address internal controls and governance that complement Basel_III Pillar_2 requirements. IFRS_9 (International Financial Reporting Standard 9) interacts with Basel_III through its expected credit loss model, which affects how banks recognize credit impairments and, consequently, their regulatory capital positions. In the data protection domain, LGPD and GDPR impose obligations on how banks handle customer data, intersecting with Basel_III Pillar_3 disclosure requirements regarding privacy considerations.

## Conclusion

Basel_III represents a comprehensive overhaul of international banking regulation by the BCBS. Through its three-pillar structure, strengthened capital and liquidity requirements, and enhanced risk coverage, Basel_III aims to build a more resilient global banking system. The framework continues to evolve as the BCBS monitors implementation and addresses emerging risks, ensuring that the regulatory architecture keeps pace with developments in the financial industry.
